You will spend your life among perhaps a few hundred people you actually know. Beyond them are eight billion others whose existence you accept as an abstraction. Almost every ethical system ever written is, at bottom, an argument about where the edge of your obligation sits — and almost none of them agree.

:::fact
Codes of hospitality binding a household to an unknown traveller appear independently in Greek, Vedic, Semitic, Celtic and West African traditions, in societies with no plausible contact between them.
:::

That convergence is striking enough that it demands explanation. The usual one is practical: in a world without inns, police or insurance, a reciprocal duty to shelter strangers is simply good infrastructure. Today I feed you; in a year, on a road far from here, someone feeds me.

:::interpretation
But the texts themselves rarely argue this way. They tend to describe hospitality not as an exchange but as a recognition — the guest is treated as though something larger had arrived with them.
:::

> The guest is not a person you are helping. The guest is a person in front of whom you find out who you are.

This is the part that survives the collapse of the practical argument. We no longer need strangers to shelter us; we have hotels, embassies, insurance. And yet the sense that turning someone away costs us something has not gone anywhere. It is worth asking what that residue is.

## The circle and its edge

Consider how the circle actually moves in practice. It does not widen through argument. It widens through proximity — a face, a name, a story told well enough that the abstraction collapses into a person. This is why famine statistics do not move us and one photograph does. It is a documented weakness in our moral equipment, and it is also, possibly, the only mechanism we have.

:::hypothesis
If moral concern expands mainly through vivid particulars rather than through reasoning, then storytelling is not decoration on ethics. It may be the delivery mechanism ethics actually runs on.
:::

:::note
Which would make the deliberate flooding of that channel — with particulars manufactured to move us toward things that do not deserve it — a serious moral problem, and not merely an aesthetic one.
:::

:::question
If your obligation to a stranger is real, is it lessened by distance? And if it is not, how does anyone live?
:::

We do not think this resolves. We think it is the kind of question you are supposed to carry rather than settle — and that a person who has genuinely carried it treats the people in front of them differently. That may be the whole of the practical content.
