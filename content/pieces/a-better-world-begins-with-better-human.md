## Blog 2 — The First Movement Toward Creation

## Prakṛti, Kāla and the Beginning of Manifestation

In the first blog of our series, we asked a very fundamental question:

What was there before creation?

According to the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, before the manifest universe appeared, there was no world of names and forms as we know it. The material principle existed in an unmanifest condition, while the Supreme Lord remained beyond material nature.

But then comes the next question:

If everything was unmanifest, what was the first movement toward creation?

The Bhāgavatam gives us a profound answer involving Prakṛti, Kāla (Time), and the Supreme Puruṣa.

## 1. Prakṛti Before Manifestation

Before manifestation, Prakṛti remains unmanifest—the potential

for material creation without differentiated forms.

The Bhāgavatam describes the primordial material condition as Pradhāna.

Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 3.26.10 says:

> यत्तत्‍त्रिगुणमव्यक्तं नित्यं सदसदात्मकम् । प्रधानं प्रकृतिं प्राहुरविशेषं विशेषवत् ॥ १० ॥

> yat tat tri-guṇam avyaktaṁ nityaṁ sad-asad-ātmakam pradhānaṁ prakṛtiṁ prāhur aviśeṣaṁ viśeṣavat

The Bhāgavatam describes Pradhāna as the unmanifest, undifferentiated condition associated with the three guṇas, from which the manifest material state arises.

This is important.

At this stage, we should not imagine planets, stars, bodies, mountains or oceans.

There are no differentiated forms yet.

The potential for material manifestation is present, but manifestation itself has not yet unfolded.

It is like a seed that contains the potential for a tree, although the tree with its branches, leaves and fruits cannot yet be seen.

The analogy is imperfect, but it helps us understand the distinction between potential existence and manifest existence.

## 2. Prakṛti and the Three Guṇas

The Bhāgavatam calls this unmanifest material principle tri-guṇam—associated with the three guṇas:

Sattva

Rajas

Tamas

At this primordial stage, they are not yet operating as the differentiated qualities that we experience in the manifest world.

There is therefore no ordinary activity, no recognizable form and no differentiated material universe.

This is why the word avyakta—unmanifest—is so significant.

Creation does not begin with a finished universe suddenly appearing.

It begins with the transition from the unmanifest toward manifestation.

## 3. Then Comes Kāla — Time

Here we reach one of the most important ideas in the Bhāgavatam's account of creation.

The material principle does not independently begin creating itself.

The Bhāgavatam describes the relationship between the Supreme Lord, material nature and Kāla, or eternal Time.

Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 3.5.26 states:

> कालवृत्त्या तु मायायां गुणमय्यामधोक्षजः । पुरुषेणात्मभूतेन वीर्यमाधत्त वीर्यवान् ॥ २६ ॥

> kāla-vṛttyā tu māyāyāṁ guṇa-mayyām adhokṣajaḥ puruṣeṇātma-bhūtena vīryam ādhatta vīryavān

The verse describes the Supreme Being, through the Puruṣa, impregnating material nature, which is constituted of the three guṇas, while eternal Time is involved in the process.

This gives us a crucial principle:

Time is not merely a measurement created after the universe exists.

In the Bhāgavatam's cosmological framework, Kāla is already involved in the movement toward manifestation.

## 4. What Does "Time Begins Creation" Mean?

We should be careful here.

The Bhāgavatam is not saying that a clock suddenly appeared.

Kāla is much deeper than the human measurement of seconds, minutes and years.

Our clocks measure change.

But in the Bhāgavatam's cosmological language, Kāla is associated with the power through which processes unfold—creation, transformation and eventual dissolution.

Without differentiation, there is no ordinary sequence of events that we can observe.

When manifestation begins, processes begin to unfold.

Potential becomes activity.

Undifferentiated nature becomes differentiated.

And the cosmic process begins.

So, for the reader, one useful way of understanding it is:

Kāla is not simply something inside creation; Kāla is one of the principles through which creation becomes a process.

## 5. The Supreme Puruṣa and Prakṛti

There is another distinction that is essential.

The Bhāgavatam does not present creation as Prakṛti acting independently.

There is the Puruṣa, the Supreme conscious principle, and there is Prakṛti, the material nature.

The Supreme Lord is described as beyond material nature, yet material nature becomes the field in which manifestation takes place.

This relationship is beautifully summarized in Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 3.26.

The chapter first explains that the Supreme Soul is beginningless and beyond the guṇas, and that material nature is accepted as the divine subtle energy characterized by the three modes.

Therefore, we should not imagine the beginning of creation as two independent forces accidentally meeting.

The Bhāgavatam presents an ordered relationship:

Supreme Lord → Puruṣa → Prakṛti → Kāla → Manifestation

This is not intended as a simple mechanical formula. It is a philosophical description of the relationship between consciousness, material potential and cosmic process.

## 6. From Stillness to Movement

Now we can understand the title of this article:

## The First Movement Toward Creation

Before manifestation, Prakṛti is unmanifest.

The guṇas are present, but the differentiated material universe has not appeared.

Then, through the Supreme Puruṣa and under the influence of Kāla, the dormant material potential becomes active.

The Bhāgavatam later describes this process in even more detail.

In Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 3.26.50–51, the primary elements are described as initially unmixed. The Lord, associated with kāla, karma and guṇa, enters the process, and the elements are then roused into activity and brought together.

This is the beginning of a chain.

The universe is not yet the universe we see.

But the process of becoming has begun.

## 7. The Next Stage: Mahat-tattva

Once Prakṛti is set into activity, the next major stage is the emergence of Mahat-tattva, the cosmic intelligence.

This is extremely important because it marks a transition from the completely undifferentiated material condition toward an organized cosmic manifestation.

The Bhāgavatam describes the first creation as the creation of Mahat, the cosmic intelligence in which the interaction of the natural qualities takes place.

So our sequence is beginning to take shape:

Unmanifest Pradhāna

↓

Influence of Kāla and the Supreme Puruṣa

↓

Prakṛti becomes active

↓

Mahat-tattva

↓

Further differentiation

↓

Cosmic manifestation

We will follow this sequence carefully in the coming blogs rather than jumping directly to planets, stars and living beings.

## 8. A Very Important Clarification

When reading this account, we should avoid immediately asking:

> "Which modern scientific object does Prakṛti represent?"

or

> "Is Kāla exactly the same as Einstein's concept of time?"

The Bhāgavatam is presenting a cosmological and metaphysical framework, not writing a modern physics textbook.

Its vocabulary—Prakṛti, Puruṣa, Kāla, guṇa, Mahat and so on—belongs to a philosophical system with its own meanings.

We can certainly compare these ideas with modern cosmology later.

But comparison should come after understanding what the Bhāgavatam itself is saying.

That distinction will be especially important throughout this series.

## 9. What Is Actually "Created" First?

Another subtle point emerges here.

The first thing created is not a planet.

Not the Sun.

Not the Earth.

Not even a human being.

The first stages concern the principles from which differentiated material existence can emerge.

The Bhāgavatam's creation account therefore moves from:

unmanifest → cosmic intelligence → ego → subtle elements → gross elements → cosmic structure → living beings and their forms.

This is why studying creation step by step is so important.

If we jump directly to the Sun or Earth, we miss the philosophical architecture of the entire account.

## 10. Life.Human Reflection — Creation Begins With Movement

There is a beautiful reflection here for human life.

Before anything becomes visible, something may already be happening within.

A thought comes before an action.

An intention comes before a project.

A seed comes before a tree.

A possibility comes before an achievement.

The Bhāgavatam's description of creation reminds us that manifestation is a process.

What we see is often only the final stage of something that began invisibly.

This also gives us a different understanding of Time.

We normally think:

> "Time is passing."

But perhaps a better question is:

> "What is Time allowing me to become?"

Every moment takes something from the realm of possibility into the realm of experience.

A thought becomes a decision.

A decision becomes an action.

Repeated action becomes a habit.

A habit becomes character.

And character gradually shapes a life.

In this sense, human life itself contains a small reflection of the cosmic principle:

potential → movement → manifestation.

## 11. How Should the Reader Understand This Series?

For these blogs, the reader should not approach the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam's creation account merely as a list of ancient claims about how the physical universe was assembled.

Instead, read it at three levels.

## First — What does the text actually say?

We will carefully follow the Sanskrit verses and their sequence.

## Second — What does the philosophy mean?

We will understand concepts such as:

Puruṣa, Prakṛti, Kāla, guṇa, Mahat, ahaṅkāra and the elements.

## Third — What can a human being learn from it?

This is where the Life.Human reflection becomes meaningful.

The purpose is not to force modern science into the Bhāgavatam, nor to reject the text simply because its framework is different from modern cosmology.

The purpose is to understand first, compare later, and reflect deeply throughout.
