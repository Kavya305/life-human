Begin with the least controversial observation available: examinations are a technology. They were designed, by identifiable people, to solve an identifiable problem. They are not a natural feature of learning, and for most of the history of education they did not exist.

:::fact
Competitive written examination as a mechanism for allocating state office was developed in imperial China and formalised over centuries; the British civil service adopted a version of it in the mid-nineteenth century, explicitly citing that precedent.
:::

:::interpretation
The problem it solved was patronage, not pedagogy. An exam is a device for making selection appear impersonal. Whether it measures capability is a separate question from whether it defeats nepotism — and it was adopted to do the second.
:::

This matters because the two purposes have long since come apart. A mechanism built to rank candidates fairly for a limited number of posts has been placed at the centre of childhood, and children are not candidates. They are people in the middle of becoming.

:::hypothesis
When a measure is used to allocate something scarce, effort migrates from the underlying quality to the measure. If that holds in education, then a system optimising examination performance will, over time, produce less of the understanding it was meant to detect.
:::

:::note
We are not arguing for the abolition of assessment. Something has to allocate places, and the alternatives to an exam have historically been worse and less fair. The question is narrower: what does it cost, who pays it, and have we ever actually looked?
:::

:::question
If a child could not be ranked, what would we want them to learn?
:::
