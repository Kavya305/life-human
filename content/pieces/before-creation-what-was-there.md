## Before Creation: What Was There?

## The question before the first moment

When we speak about creation, our natural question is:

How did the universe begin?

We may imagine a beginning somewhere in the distant past—perhaps a first explosion, a first particle, a first movement, or a first moment of time.

But the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam asks a question even deeper than that:

Before there was a universe, what was there?

Before the Earth existed.

Before the Sun and Moon appeared.

Before the stars and planets.

Before Brahmā began his work of creation.

Before the material elements became manifest.

Before the universe had taken any visible form.

What was there?

The answer given by the Bhāgavatam is not "nothing."

It begins with the Supreme Reality.

## 1. The Bhāgavatam begins with the ultimate cause

The very first verse of the Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam establishes the foundation for everything that follows.

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 1.1.1

> जन्माद्यस्य यतोऽन्वयादितरतश्चार्थेष्वभिज्ञः स्वराट् । तेने ब्रह्म हृदा य आदिकवये मुह्यन्ति यत्सूरयः । तेजोवारिमृदां यथा विनिमयो यत्र त्रिसर्गोऽमृषा । धाम्ना स्वेन सदा निरस्तकुहकं सत्यं परं धीमहि ॥

janmādy asya yato 'nvayād itarataś cārtheṣv abhijñaḥ svarāṭ

tene brahma hṛdā ya ādi-kavaye muhyanti yat sūrayaḥ

tejo-vāri-mṛdāṁ yathā vinimayo yatra tri-sargo 'mṛṣā

dhāmnā svena sadā nirasta-kuhakaṁ satyaṁ paraṁ dhīmahi

The opening verse identifies the Absolute Truth as the source of the creation, its maintenance and its dissolution. It describes that Truth as fully conscious and independent—svarāṭ, one who is independent and has no higher cause.

In other words, the Bhāgavatam does not begin its cosmology with matter.

It begins with the source of matter.

It does not begin with the universe.

It begins with the reality from which the universe comes.

This distinction is fundamental to understanding everything that follows.

## 2. The clearest answer: "I alone existed"

The most direct answer to our question appears in the revelation given by the Supreme Lord to Brahmā.

##

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 2.9.33

> अहमेवासमेवाग्रे नान्यद् यत् सदसत् परम् । पश्चादहं यदेतच्च योऽवशिष्येत सोऽस्म्यहम् ॥

aham evāsam evāgre

nānyad yat sad-asat param

paścād ahaṁ yad etac ca

yo 'vaśiṣyeta so 'smy aham

The Lord says, in essence:

> "I existed before creation, when there was nothing but Myself. Nor was material nature, the cause of this creation, present. What you see now is also Me, and after annihilation, what remains will also be Me."

This is perhaps the most important verse for our entire journey.

Notice the sequence:

Before creation — I existed.

During creation — I am present.

After annihilation — I remain.

Creation therefore does not bring the Supreme Being into existence.

Rather, creation comes into manifestation from that Supreme Reality.

## 3. Does "nothing else" mean absolute nothingness?

This point requires care.

The verse says:

> nānyad yat sad-asat param

There was no other material cause or effect beyond the Lord.

And the verse specifically adds:

> Nor was there material nature, the cause of this creation.

So when the Bhāgavatam says that before creation there was "nothing but Myself," it is not presenting a modern philosophical idea of absolute nothingness.

It is describing the condition before the material cosmos was manifested.

There was no material universe as we know it.

There was no Earth.

No Sun.

No Moon.

No stars.

No planetary systems.

No gross material elements in their manifested cosmic arrangement.

And, importantly, material nature itself was not functioning as the manifested cause of the universe.

The Supreme Lord, however, was.

This is why the first verse calls Him:

> svarāṭ — completely independent.

Everything else depends upon Him; He does not depend upon something else.

## 4. Creation does not make the Lord; creation manifests His energy

This gives us an important way of understanding the word creation.

When we normally say that something is created, we often imagine that something previously nonexistent suddenly becomes existent.

But the Bhāgavatam's cosmology is more subtle.

The material universe becomes manifest.

Its elements, forms, beings and activities become perceptible and organized.

The Lord is the ultimate source and controller of this manifestation.

The Bhāgavatam later states:

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 2.10.12

> द्रव्यं कर्म च कालश्च स्वभावो जीव एव च । यदनुग्रहतः सन्ति न सन्ति यदुपेक्षया ॥

dravyaṁ karma ca kālaś ca

svabhāvo jīva eva ca

yad-anugrahataḥ santi

na santi yad-upekṣayā

The verse explains that material ingredients, activities, time, the modes/nature, and the living entities exist by the Lord's arrangement and mercy; without His sanction, they do not exist in the manifested sense.

So our first picture of creation should not be:

Nothing → Universe

It should be understood more carefully as:

Supreme Reality → manifestation of the material cosmos

## 5. The Lord is not merely "somewhere before the universe"

There is another profound point in the four verses given to Brahmā.

The Lord says:

> "What you see now is also I."

This means that the Supreme Reality is not only relevant to the beginning.

The Lord is the foundation of:

before creation

the manifested universe

and

after dissolution.

This gives us a three-stage perspective:

## Before

The material universe is unmanifest.

## During

The universe becomes manifest and functions through its various energies, elements, beings and laws.

## After

The material manifestation is withdrawn.

But the Supreme Reality remains.

This is why the Bhāgavatam asks us to look beyond temporary forms toward the underlying Truth.

## 6. What is Māyā in this picture?

The next verse is particularly important.

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 2.9.34

> ऋतेऽर्थं यत् प्रतीयेत न प्रतीयेत चात्मनि । तद्विद्यादात्मनो मायां यथाभासो यथा तमः ॥

ṛte 'rthaṁ yat pratīyeta

na pratīyeta cātmani

tad vidyād ātmano māyāṁ

yathābhāso yathā tamaḥ

The Lord teaches Brahmā that whatever appears to exist independently of Him should be understood as His māyā, like a reflection or darkness.

This does not simply mean that everything we experience is imaginary.

The important point is the word independently.

A thing may be real as part of the created order, while our understanding of it becomes illusory when we imagine that it exists completely independently of its source.

For example:

A tree exists.

A human body exists.

A planet exists.

A civilization exists.

But according to the Bhāgavatam, none of these possesses independent existence.

Their existence is dependent.

This distinction will become increasingly important as we move through the stages of creation.

## 7. The Lord is both within creation and beyond it

The next verse takes the idea even further.

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 2.9.35

> यथा महान्ति भूतानि भूतेषूच्चावचेष्वनु । प्रविष्टान्यप्रविष्टानि तथा तेषु न तेष्वहम् ॥

yathā mahānti bhūtāni

bhūteṣūccāvaceṣv anu

praviṣṭāny apraviṣṭāni

tathā teṣu na teṣv aham

The Lord explains that just as the great material elements enter into all kinds of created forms while also remaining beyond those individual forms, He is present within everything created and yet is also beyond everything created.

This gives us another essential principle:

The universe is dependent upon the Lord, but the Lord is not dependent upon the universe.

That is why we cannot simply identify the universe itself with the Supreme Reality.

The source is greater than the manifestation.

## 8. The conclusion: inquire into what remains true everywhere

The final verse of this instruction is especially beautiful.

## Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam 2.9.36

> एतावदेव जिज्ञास्यं तत्त्वजिज्ञासुनात्मनः । अन्वयव्यतिरेकाभ्यां यत् स्यात् सर्वत्र सर्वदा ॥

etāvad eva jijñāsyaṁ

tattva-jijñāsunātmanaḥ

anvaya-vyatirekābhyāṁ

yat syāt sarvatra sarvadā

The essential teaching is:

> One who is searching for the Absolute Truth should inquire into that which remains true directly and indirectly, everywhere and at all times.

And this gives us the method for our entire exploration of creation.

We should not ask only:

"What appeared?"

We should also ask:

"What was present before it?"

"What sustains it?"

"What remains when it disappears?"

If something exists only for a temporary period, while another reality remains before, during and after it, then the Bhāgavatam asks us to distinguish between the temporary manifestation and the deeper reality behind it.

## 9. So what was there at "zero"?

If we reduce everything to the simplest possible picture, according to these verses:

## Before material creation:

The Supreme Lord existed.

There was no manifested material universe.

There was no manifested material nature functioning as the cause of the cosmos.

There were no planets, stars, Earth or Sun.

There was no Brahmā performing his cosmic creative function.

The material creation had not yet appeared.

And therefore, the beginning of our story is not:

> "Once there was nothing."

It is:

> "Before the material manifestation, the Supreme Reality already existed."

That is the Bhāgavatam's starting point.

## 10. But this is only the beginning

Now a new question naturally arises.

If the material universe was not yet manifest, then:

How did manifestation begin?

What is Prakṛti?

What is Kāla, or Time?

How does the Lord's energy become activated?

What is the first principle that becomes manifest?

What is Mahat-tattva?

And how do the subtle principles eventually become the gross elements from which the universe is constructed?

These are not questions we should answer yet.

That is important.

We should not jump from "God existed" directly to "the Earth was created."

The Bhāgavatam describes an ordered sequence.

Our next step will therefore be:

> From the unmanifest condition to the first stage of cosmic manifestation.

That is where the discussion of Prakṛti, Kāla and Mahat-tattva begins.

## Life.Human Reflection

## Before the universe, there was not "nothing."

Perhaps one of the most powerful lessons in this opening is hidden in the question itself.

Human beings are often frightened by emptiness.

We think:

"If I lose this, what will remain?"

We identify ourselves with our possessions, our position, our body, our relationships, our achievements and even our present circumstances.

But the Bhāgavatam asks us to look at existence differently.

Forms appear.

Forms change.

Forms disappear.

Yet the question remains:

What is the reality that does not disappear when the forms disappear?

The universe itself, according to the Bhāgavatam, is temporary in its manifested form.

Our bodies are temporary.

Our circumstances are temporary.

Our successes are temporary.

Our failures are temporary.

Even civilizations and worlds have their periods of manifestation and dissolution.

So perhaps the deeper human question is not:

> "How can I make my temporary existence permanent?"

but:

> "What is the eternal reality upon which my temporary existence depends?"

This changes the way we look at life.

We stop seeing ourselves as isolated beings accidentally appearing in an empty universe.

Instead, we begin to ask:

Where do I come from?

What sustains me?

What remains when everything temporary changes?

And ultimately:

What is my relationship with that Reality?

That is where cosmology becomes more than cosmology.

It becomes a question about life itself.

## How Should the Reader Understand These Blogs?

This is especially important for our entire Life.Human series.

The reader should not approach these articles merely as a list of ancient claims that must either be blindly accepted or immediately rejected.

Instead, we should invite the reader to walk through the Bhāgavatam's own system of thought.

There should be four levels of understanding.

## 1. First: What does the text actually say?

We will give the relevant Sanskrit verse and explain its direct meaning.

We should not change the verse to make it fit modern science.

## 2. Second: What does the Bhāgavatam mean by its terminology?

Words such as:

Prakṛti, Kāla, Mahat-tattva, Sarga, Visarga, Loka, Jīva

have specific meanings within the Bhāgavatam's worldview.

We should understand those meanings before comparing them with modern concepts.

## 3. Third: What is interpretation?

Where the text leaves room for philosophical interpretation, we should clearly say so.

We should distinguish:

"The verse says..."

from

"The traditional interpretation is..."

and from

"One possible interpretation is..."

This will keep the blog intellectually honest.

## 4. Fourth: What does it mean for a human being?

This is where Life.Human comes in.

We are not studying creation merely to know what happened billions or trillions of years ago.

We are asking:

> What does this understanding of existence teach a human being about life?

That should be the thread running through the entire series.

## Our guiding principle

So throughout these blogs, I suggest we use this simple pattern:

VERSE → MEANING → COSMIC SEQUENCE → QUESTIONS → LIFE.HUMAN REFLECTION

And one more rule:

> We will never force an ancient verse to prove a modern scientific theory, and we will never dismiss an ancient idea merely because it does not resemble modern science.

First, we will understand the Bhāgavatam on its own terms.

Then we can compare it with modern astronomy, cosmology, philosophy or science—carefully and separately.

That way, the reader is not told what to think.

The reader is taught how to inquire.

And that, fittingly, is where the Bhāgavatam itself leaves us:

> “That which is true everywhere and at all times—seek to know that.”
