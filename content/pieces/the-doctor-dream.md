Education & Competition Series - Part 1/6

## The Doctor Dream: What Are We Really Competing For?

## NEET, parental ambition, status, money and the competition for medical seats

In Bharat, becoming a doctor is much more than choosing a profession. For many families, it represents status, security, social respect and the possibility of a high income.

But what happens when millions of young people compete for a relatively small number of medical seats?

The numbers tell an important story.

In NEET UG 2025, 22.76 lakh candidates registered and 22,09,318 appeared. Of those who appeared, 12,36,531 were declared qualified for admission to undergraduate medical and allied courses. (Indian Express)

But this is where an important distinction must be made:

Qualifying NEET does not mean getting an MBBS seat.

For the 2025–26 academic year, India had 1,28,875 undergraduate MBBS seats in government and private medical colleges. (Digital Sansad)

So we are looking at approximately:

22.1 lakh — NEET candidates who appeared

12.4 lakh — NEET candidates who qualified

1.29 lakh — MBBS seats

These numbers should not be interpreted as a simple admission ratio because counselling involves different categories, quotas, choices, and other courses. But they clearly demonstrate the extraordinary scarcity of MBBS seats relative to the number of aspirants.

## And how large is the Class 12 population?

Here too, we need to be precise.

India does not have one national Class 12 examination. Students graduate through CBSE, ISC and numerous state and other boards, each with its own examination and result system.

UDISE+ reported about 2.76 crore students enrolled in Classes 11–12 in 2024–25. Since that represents two years of schooling, it gives us a broad sense of the enormous size of the higher-secondary population, but it should not be divided mechanically by two and called the annual number of Class 12 graduates. (Scribd)

For perspective, CBSE alone had about 16.93 lakh students appear for Class 12 in 2025, of whom 14.96 lakh passed. (Careers360)

Therefore, rather than claiming that a precise number of students “pass Class 12 every year in Bharat,” it is more accurate to say that tens of lakhs of students complete Class 12 across India's many boards each year, while more than 22 lakh candidates competed in NEET UG 2025.

## So why does medicine become such a powerful aspiration?

There is an obvious answer:

Medicine is a difficult profession that requires intelligence, discipline and years of training.

For many students, becoming a doctor is genuinely about science, healing and service.

But that may not be the entire story.

A medical career can also bring:

STATUS.

SOCIAL RESPECT.

SECURITY.

AND POTENTIALLY HIGH INCOME.

There is nothing wrong with wanting financial security for one's family. There is nothing wrong with ambition either.

The uncomfortable question is different:

> If becoming a doctor offered the same social prestige but only an ordinary, comfortable income, would the same proportion of parents still insist that their children become doctors?

We cannot know the answer without evidence.

But the question is worth asking.

Because when status + money + security + scarcity become concentrated around one profession, competition naturally becomes intense.

And when competition becomes extreme, another industry grows around it:

## Coaching.

Parents spend heavily. Students spend years preparing. Families make enormous sacrifices.

And where the rewards are enormous and opportunities are scarce, the temptation to find shortcuts can also become enormous.

This does not mean that parents are greedy.

It does not mean that students are dishonest.

And it certainly does not mean that doctors enter medicine only for money.

It means something simpler:

> Human beings respond to incentives.

If society creates extraordinary rewards around a profession while providing relatively few opportunities to enter it, we should expect extraordinary competition.

The important question, therefore, is not simply:

“Who cheated?”

It is also:

“What incentives created such intense pressure in the first place?”

And that takes us beyond NEET.

Perhaps we need to examine the entire structure:

How many medical seats should Bharat have?

Why are private medical seats so expensive?

Why has coaching become so powerful?

How much of medical income comes from genuine professional value, and how much is created by scarcity?

And how can we create a system where becoming a doctor is driven primarily by aptitude and the desire to serve—not merely by status and financial rewards?

The answer isn't to blame parents.

It isn't to blame students.

It isn't even to blame doctors.

The deeper question is about incentives, scarcity and the design of the system itself.

Because if we want different behaviour, perhaps we need to create different incentives.

> Human behaviour is shaped by incentives. If we want better outcomes, we must design better systems.

And perhaps that is the real question NEET is asking Indian society:

## Are we producing doctors—or are we producing a competition for the status of being a doctor?
