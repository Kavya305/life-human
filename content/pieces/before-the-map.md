The difficulty begins with the words. Almost every term available for describing the deep past of the subcontinent — nation, religion, race, empire, even culture — was formalised in nineteenth-century Europe for nineteenth-century European purposes. Using them is not neutral. It imports a set of assumptions about how human groups work, and then finds those assumptions everywhere it looks.

:::fact
The urban settlements of the Indus valley — Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira and roughly a thousand identified sites — show standardised weights, fired brick in consistent ratios, and drainage infrastructure, across an area larger than contemporary Egypt and Mesopotamia combined.
:::

:::fact
The Indus script remains undeciphered. There is no agreed reading of any inscription, and no confirmed bilingual text.
:::

:::interpretation
The absence of monumental palaces or unambiguous royal iconography has been read by many archaeologists as evidence of a less centralised political order than its contemporaries. This is a reading of an absence, and absences are the weakest form of evidence.
:::

:::hypothesis
Some scholars propose administrative continuity between Indus urban practice and later South Asian civic forms. The material chain is not established, and the proposal should be treated as open.
:::

:::note
We are marking these distinctions deliberately, and we will keep marking them. The deep history of this region is a place where confident assertion has done a great deal of damage in several directions at once.
:::

What we can say is narrower and, we think, more interesting: a very large number of people organised a very large area for a very long time, and left behind almost no evidence of the thing we have been trained to look for first — a ruler.

:::question
What would we notice about the past if we were not, by habit, looking for kings?
:::
