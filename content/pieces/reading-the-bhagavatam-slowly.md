There is a way of approaching an old religious text that flatters everyone and teaches nothing: treat it as a storehouse of pleasant sayings, extract the ones that agree with you, and leave. It is done reverently and dismissively in almost equal measure, and both versions come to the same result — the text never gets to say anything the reader did not already believe.

We want to try the harder thing. Read it slowly. Let it be strange. Notice where it is beautiful, where it is difficult, where it contradicts itself, and where it is doing something we would not do.

:::fact
The Bhāgavata Purāṇa runs to roughly eighteen thousand verses across twelve books, and exists in multiple recensions with meaningful textual variation.
:::

:::interpretation
Its structure is recursive — narrators telling of narrators telling of narrators. That framing is not incidental decoration. It appears to be making a claim about how knowledge reaches a person: always through someone, never neat.
:::

:::note
A note on what this series is not. It is not devotional instruction, and it is not a debunking. Both would be easier. We are trying to read a text that a great many people have lived inside, carefully enough that they would recognise it, and honestly enough that someone outside it learns something real.
:::

:::question
Can you take a text seriously without agreeing with it? Most of us have never been shown how.
:::
