## If you're absent from the table where a revolution is shaped, you may end up as a tool in someone else's quest for power.

Every revolution has two stages. The first takes place on the streets, where people protest, organize, and challenge the existing system. The second takes place around tables—where constitutions are written, policies are decided, institutions are designed, and the future direction of society is determined.

Most people participate in the first stage. Very few influence the second.

That is the warning behind this quote.

Being physically present in a movement is not the same as helping shape its vision. History shows that those who control the ideas, negotiations, and institutions often determine the future, while those who only provide numbers and enthusiasm may have little influence once the struggle is over.

The "table" in this quote is symbolic. It represents every place where important decisions are made—whether it is a constitutional assembly, a parliament, a village council, a company boardroom, a university committee, or even a family discussion. If decisions affecting your future are always made without your participation, you surrender your voice before the outcome is even announced.

History offers many reminders.

During the American Revolution, military victory alone did not create the United States. The Constitutional Convention became just as important as the battlefield. Leaders debated the separation of powers, checks and balances, and the protection of individual rights. The future was shaped not only by those who fought but also by those who participated in designing institutions.

Contrast this with several twentieth-century revolutions where a small group gradually concentrated decision-making within itself. Many ordinary supporters believed they were building a more just society, but because they had little influence over the institutions that emerged, they eventually found themselves governed by another centralized authority.

The lesson extends far beyond politics.

In a company, employees may work hard to build success, but if they never contribute ideas or participate in important discussions, others decide the organization's direction.

In a community, citizens who never attend meetings or vote often complain about decisions they had no role in making.

Even within families, relationships become healthier when everyone has a chance to express their views instead of allowing one voice to dominate every decision.

Participation is therefore more than attendance. It means understanding issues, asking thoughtful questions, offering ideas, accepting responsibility, and remaining engaged after the excitement fades.

At the same time, participation should never become blind loyalty. Healthy societies need disagreement, debate, and accountability. A table where everyone agrees out of fear is not a place of freedom—it is merely another form of control.

This quote is not encouraging everyone to seek power. It is encouraging everyone to become a responsible citizen rather than a passive spectator.

When important decisions are made without informed public participation, power naturally gathers in fewer hands. But when ordinary people educate themselves, think critically, and contribute constructively, revolutions are more likely to produce lasting institutions instead of temporary excitement.

## Life.Human Reflection

Real change is not created only by those who raise slogans. It is also created by those who help shape ideas, values, and institutions.

Before lending your energy to any cause, ask yourself:

Do I understand its vision?

Am I free to question it?

Is there space for ordinary people to participate in shaping the future?

Because if you are absent from the table where the future is being designed, someone else will design that future for you.
